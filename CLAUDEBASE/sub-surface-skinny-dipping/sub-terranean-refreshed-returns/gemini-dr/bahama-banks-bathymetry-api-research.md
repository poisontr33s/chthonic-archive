# **Hydrospatial Resolution and API Integration Strategy for Volumetric Rendering of the Bahama Banks**

The synthesis of physically accurate oceanic environments within a custom Rust/Vulkan rendering architecture relies entirely on the precise calibration of optical light transport models against high-fidelity geospatial data. When computing the volumetric properties of water using the Beer–Lambert law, the absorption and scattering of light across specific wavelengths are governed by the physical depth of the water column. In a rendering pipeline calibrated to empirical oceanic optics—specifically the Jerlov IB water type with measured diffuse attenuation coefficients (![][image1]) at 670 nm, 550 nm, and 440 nm equal to 0.471 m⁻¹, 0.076 m⁻¹, and 0.050 m⁻¹ respectively—the mathematical accuracy of the underlying bathymetric grid is the single most critical dependency for visual realism.  
The primary architectural defect currently identified in the Nassau and Great Bahama Bank rendering pipeline is a severe spatial resolution deficit. The 0.5 to 2.0-meter shallow tidal flats of the Nassau Bank are being computationally homogenized into 5 to 10-meter channels. Because the attenuation of the red spectrum (670 nm) is highly aggressive in the water column, an averaged depth of 8 meters ensures near-total absorption of red light. This spatial aliasing destroys the high-albedo turquoise backscatter required for photorealism, forcing the volumetric shader to render the shallow banks with the deep, saturated blues characteristic of the open ocean.  
This exhaustive research report investigates the geomorphological spatial frequencies of the Bahama Banks to define the exact grid resolution threshold necessary to resolve the shallow flats. It evaluates the capabilities of keyless queryable application programming interfaces (APIs) such as Open Topo Data, assesses the viability of recently published global grids including the GEBCO 2024 release and NOAA’s ETOPO 2022, and identifies high-resolution, open-access Satellite Derived Bathymetry (SDB) products capable of bridging the identified resolution gap to unlock the correct optical rendering of the Bahamian shallows.

The visual identity of the Bahama Banks—the vibrant, luminous turquoise—is produced by a specific balance of spectral transmission reflecting off the highly reflective white oolitic sand and aragonite mud that forms the bottom albedo1.  
The critical factor is the survival of the red spectrum (670 nm), which possesses an aggressive attenuation coefficient of ![][image6] in Jerlov IB waters. At a true physical depth of 1.0 meter, the transmission of red light down to the seafloor and back to the surface (a 2.0-meter total path length) allows a significant percentage of the red spectrum to escape the water column, mixing with the highly transmissive green and blue wavelengths to produce the characteristic turquoise hue. However, when the bathymetric grid artificially averages a 1.0-meter flat and a surrounding 15.0-meter channel into a uniform 8.0-meter cell, the total path length for the light ray becomes 16.0 meters.

As demonstrated by the transmission calculations, an averaged depth of 8.0 meters leaves only 0.05% of the red light intact, completely extinguishing the spectral frequencies required to render the shallow banks. Therefore, fixing the bathymetric input is not merely a geometric enhancement; it is an absolute optical necessity for the shader to function.  
To determine the minimum acceptable grid resolution, the physical dimensions of the geomorphological features must be established. The Bahama Banks are uniquely characterized by extreme bathymetric contrasts. The Great Bahama Canyon, encompassing the Tongue of the Ocean and the Northwest Providence Channel, exhibits a V-shaped cross-section with sheer walls dropping sharply to 4,500 meters1. The canyon spans approximately 250 kilometers in length and reaches widths of 37 kilometers, dissecting the shallow platforms1.  
Adjacent to these abyssal trenches lie the shallow carbonate platforms, which are dominated by tidal sand ridges, oolitic shoals, and extensive mud flats4. The tidal sand ridges on the margins, such as those near the Joulters Cays, average over 10 kilometers in length but are frequently only a few hundred meters wide5. These ridges are separated by deeper tidal channels that average over 1.2 kilometers in width and can reach depths of up to 8 meters5.  
According to the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem, accurately capturing a geomorphological feature requires a spatial sampling interval at least half the size of the feature's shortest dimension. To resolve a 200-meter-wide shallow sand ridge distinctly from an adjacent 8-meter-deep channel, a maximum grid resolution of 100 meters is mathematically required. If the grid resolution exceeds 200 meters, the sampling process will inevitably blend the 1-meter shoal and the 8-meter channel into a single pixel. Consequently, the answer to the specific architectural query—at what grid resolution the 0.5 to 2.0-meter flats start to appear as distinct cells—is firmly established between 50 meters and 100 meters. Any spatial interval coarser than 150 meters will result in destructive geomorphological aliasing.

## **Diagnostics of the Current Open Topo Data Pipeline**

The existing data ingestion pipeline utilizes the Open Topo Data API to extract elevations from the GEBCO 2020 dataset, yielding an effective resolution of approximately 0.3 degrees, which translates to roughly 30-kilometer cells. A critical diagnostic analysis of this workflow reveals two distinct layers of failure: extreme under-sampling on the client side and the inherent limitations of the API itself.  
The native resolution of the GEBCO 2020 dataset is 15 arc-seconds, which equates to approximately 450 meters at the equator6. The 30-kilometer cell size currently observed in the pipeline strongly indicates a sampling error within the data-gathering script. The Open Topo Data API accepts an array of latitude and longitude coordinates and returns the elevation at each point by interpolating the underlying raster dataset7. The API supports nearest, bilinear, and cubic interpolation algorithms7. If the pipeline is casting a 56 by 22 grid of query points across the entire Bahamian bounding box, it is artificially decimating the 450-meter GEBCO data down to 30 kilometers, thereby throwing away 98% of the available spatial information. Rectifying the script to query the API at 450-meter intervals would significantly sharpen the boundaries of the Great Bahama Canyon, though it would remain insufficient for the 100-meter threshold required for the tidal flats.  
However, scaling the point-query script to request a dense 100-meter grid across the entire 20°–27°N by 79.9°–72.7°W bounding box exposes the limitations of the Open Topo Data public infrastructure. The free public instance of this API (api.opentopodata.org) is designed as a lightweight tool for localized elevation checks, acting as an open-source alternative to the Google Maps Elevation API9. To maintain server stability, the public service strictly enforces aggressive rate limits: a maximum of 100 locations per request, one call per second, and a hard cap of 1,000 calls per day10. Extracting a continuous, high-resolution bathymetric mesh covering tens of thousands of square kilometers via this REST architecture would require millions of discrete coordinate queries, rendering it entirely incompatible with an automated rendering pipeline.  
Furthermore, the Open Topo Data service has not updated its backend repository to reflect modern global bathymetry. The public endpoint exclusively hosts the GEBCO 2020 dataset, utilizing a Python and GDAL Virtual Format (VRT) backend to add 5-pixel overlap buffers to the raw NetCDF/GeoTIFF tiles provided by the IHO/IOC6. Despite GEBCO releasing successive annual updates, the maintainers of the Open Topo Data public API have not upgraded the service to the GEBCO 2024 release10. While the source code allows developers to self-host the server via Docker and manually load newer datasets, relying on the public API inherently locks the pipeline to outdated 2020 data.

## **Evaluation of the GEBCO 2024 Global Grid**

The General Bathymetric Chart of the Oceans (GEBCO) is a continuous terrain model for ocean and land, operating under the joint auspices of the International Hydrographic Organization (IHO) and the Intergovernmental Oceanographic Commission (IOC)12. As the authoritative source for global bathymetry, its latest iteration, the GEBCO 2024 Grid, was officially released in July 202412.  
An analysis of the GEBCO 2024 specifications reveals significant improvements in data coverage, yet critical limitations in spatial frequency. The GEBCO 2024 grid maintains the legacy spatial resolution of 15 arc-seconds (approximately 450 meters) on a geographic latitude and longitude grid, resulting in a matrix of 43,200 rows by 86,400 columns12. The dataset utilizes Version 2.6 of the SRTM15+ dataset as its baseline terrain model for regions between 50° South and 60° North12. This base grid is subsequently augmented with modern gridded bathymetric datasets compiled primarily from shipborne multibeam sonar data provided by four Seabed 2030 Regional Centers12.  
The compilation process utilizes a highly sophisticated "remove-restore" blending procedure. Outside of polar regions, the Regional Centers provide sparse grids where only cells containing empirical measurements are populated12. The global center computes the difference between the new multibeam data and the SRTM15+ base grid, interpolates the difference, and seamlessly adds it back to the base to minimize perturbation13. The GEBCO 2024 release successfully integrated an additional 4.34 million square kilometers of newly mapped seabed compared to the 2023 release, meaning 26.1% of the global ocean floor is now mapped with direct measurements13. The data is distributed alongside a Type Identifier (TID) Grid, which encodes a single-byte integer for every pixel to identify its source—ranging from multibeam echo-sounders (TID 11\) to predicted depths based on satellite-derived gravity data (TID 40\)13.  
Despite these advanced compilation techniques, upgrading the rendering pipeline to GEBCO 2024 will not solve the visual extinction of the turquoise shallows. The Great Bahama Bank presents immense navigational hazards for deep-draft surveying vessels due to its 0.5 to 2.0-meter depths and pervasive coral heads. Consequently, massive expanses of the Bahamian platform remain unmapped by modern multibeam sonar. In the absence of direct measurements, global grids like GEBCO are forced to interpolate these shallow areas using predicted depths guided by satellite altimetry and gravity anomaly data14. Satellite gravimetry fundamentally lacks the spatial resolution to detect micro-topographical features such as 200-meter-wide tidal sand ridges or localized mudflats. Even where high-quality data exists, the hard limit of the 15 arc-second (450-meter) grid interval inherently guarantees that the shallow flats and deep channels will be mathematically blended into 5 to 10-meter cells.

## **Evaluation of NOAA High-Resolution Bathymetric Products**

Given that 450-meter global grids are mathematically incapable of resolving the required features, the pipeline must seek out regional, high-resolution models. The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) operates the National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI), which hosts several advanced topographic-bathymetric digital elevation models17.

### **ETOPO 2022**

The ETOPO Global Relief Model is NOAA's flagship continuous terrain dataset. Historically, the legacy ETOPO1 model utilized a 1 arc-minute resolution (approximately 1.8 kilometers), making it vastly inferior to GEBCO19. However, the modernized ETOPO 2022 model significantly upgraded the resolution to 15 arc-seconds, bringing it to parity with GEBCO and SRTM15+18. ETOPO 2022 integrates numerous airborne lidar, satellite-derived topography, and shipborne bathymetry datasets18. A notable advancement in ETOPO 2022 is the rigorous use of bare-earth elevation photons from NASA's ICESat-2 satellite mission to independently validate both the input datasets and the final merged grid, resulting in a global Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of 7.17 meters18.  
Despite its superior validation methodology, ETOPO 2022 is restricted to the same 15 arc-second spatial interval as GEBCO. Therefore, it suffers from the identical geomorphological aliasing problem over the Bahama Banks and cannot serve as the primary data source for the volumetric shader.

### **Coastal Relief Models and BlueTopo**

NOAA produces higher-resolution datasets intended for coastal inundation modeling, tsunami forecasting, and precise benthic habitat mapping. The Coastal Relief Model (CRM) integrates high-resolution topographic and bathymetric data at a 1 arc-second (approximately 30-meter) resolution22. The CRM was significantly updated in 2023, encompassing regions such as the United States East Coast, the Gulf of Mexico, Puerto Rico, and Hawaii22. The CRM relies heavily on post-processed airborne bathymetric lidar and dense multibeam surveys to achieve sub-meter vertical accuracy22.  
Simultaneously, the National Bathymetric Source (NBS) project has developed BlueTopo, a nationwide model of the seafloor combining the best available hydrographic surveys into multilayer floating-point GeoTIFFs24. BlueTopo provides unparalleled spatial resolution, often reaching 2 to 10 meters, complete with vertical uncertainty metrics and source metadata embedded in Raster Attribute Tables25.  
However, a strict geographical limitation renders both the CRM and BlueTopo unviable for the Bahama Banks rendering pipeline. The high-resolution source data utilized by NOAA strictly terminates at the boundary of the United States Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) and its incorporated territories22. While the generalized rectangular bounding box for CRM Volumes 1-5 (-161° to \-64.25°W, 17° to 46°N) theoretically encompasses the latitude and longitude of the Bahamas, the high-resolution data within that grid explicitly cuts off at the Straits of Florida22. For international waters and sovereign territories like the Commonwealth of The Bahamas, the CRM and BlueTopo models seamlessly degrade back into coarser global baselines—such as ETOPO 2022 or the Global Multi-Resolution Topography (GMRT) synthesis—to fill the void22. Querying these NOAA models for the Bahamian bounding box will only return resampled 450-meter data, offering no distinct advantage over standard GEBCO files.

## **The Satellite Derived Bathymetry (SDB) Breakthrough**

To achieve sub-100-meter bathymetry in international shallow-water environments where acoustic multibeam surveys are economically or logistically prohibitive, the hydrospatial industry has pivoted to Satellite Derived Bathymetry (SDB)28. SDB represents a paradigm shift, bypassing acoustic physics entirely in favor of optical remote sensing.

### **Physics-Based Multispectral Inversion**

SDB leverages passive multispectral optical imagery—primarily acquired from the European Space Agency's Sentinel-2 constellation or high-resolution commercial satellites like Pleiades-1 and Maxar—to estimate water depth29. The methodology is founded on the same Beer-Lambert principles that drive the volumetric water shader: water absorbs and scatters light proportionally to depth across different wavelengths29. Because the red spectrum attenuates rapidly while the blue and green spectra penetrate deeply, empirical and physics-based algorithms can invert the ratio of multispectral bands returning from the seafloor to calculate the absolute depth of the water column29.  
The Bahama Banks possess the optimal optical conditions for SDB deployment. The region is largely devoid of terrigenous sediment runoff and riverine discharge, resulting in exceptionally clear oceanic water where Secchi disk depths frequently reach 30 to 40 meters30. Furthermore, the seafloor is dominated by highly reflective white carbonate sand, oolitic shoals, and reef structures, providing a robust bottom albedo signal that is easily detectable from orbit1.  
The primary limitation of passive optical SDB in the Bahamas is the episodic occurrence of "whitings"—vast patches of milky-white water caused by the suspension of fine-grained aragonite mud. These events, often spanning hundreds of square kilometers west of Andros Island, are theorized to be driven by Langmuir supercells triggered by strong winds2. Because the suspended sediment drastically elevates particulate backscattering (peaking at 490 nm) and reduces light attenuation depth, whitings completely obscure the seafloor from passive satellites2. Consequently, the generation of a clean SDB model requires the synthesis of hundreds of satellite scenes over multi-year periods to filter out atmospheric haze, cloud cover, and episodic turbidity events30.

### **Active Lidar Calibration via ICESat-2**

While passive multispectral imagery provides dense horizontal spatial coverage, its depth derivation requires rigorous vertical calibration. Historically, this required in-situ soundings. Modern SDB methodologies utilize spaceborne active lidar for absolute vertical control28. NASA's Ice, Cloud, and Land Elevation Satellite-2 (ICESat-2), launched in 2018, carries the Advanced Topographic Laser Altimeter System (ATLAS)29. ATLAS is a photon-counting green (532 nm) lidar system that not only maps terrestrial and glacial topography but exhibits remarkable water-penetrating capabilities28.  
By applying deep learning and density-based algorithms to the ATL03 Geolocated Photon data product, analysts can filter out solar background noise and water surface returns to extract highly accurate seafloor profiles down to 38 meters in clear waters28. When these ICESat-2 bathymetric transects are superimposed over the passive Sentinel-2 multispectral grids, the depth inversion algorithms are firmly calibrated. This fusion of active lidar and passive multispectral data yields SDB models with an average vertical Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of approximately 0.35 to 0.89 meters when validated against traditional airborne lidar28.

## **The TCarta 100m SDB Solution**

The specific answer to the architectural requirement for a sub-kilometer, open-access bathymetric product covering the 20°–27°N, 79.9°–72.7°W bounding box is found in the commercial sector. TCarta Marine, a global provider of hydrospatial earth observation products, has executed massive SDB processing campaigns across the Caribbean31.  
TCarta processed over 1,000 satellite scenes of 10-meter native resolution Sentinel-2 A/B imagery acquired between 2017 and 202131. By utilizing this vast temporal dataset, TCarta successfully filtered out localized turbidity and cloud cover to produce a seamless, multi-temporal composite depth map of the entire Lucayan Archipelago31. The vertical calibration was achieved using millions of data points from NASA's ICESat-2 sensor28.  
While TCarta’s 2-meter and 10-meter resolution products are retained as commercial assets for coastal engineering, aquaculture, and offshore wind farm planning, the company has released a decimated version to the public30. The "100m Satellite Derived Bathymetry Bahamas" dataset is freely available as an open-access layer on the ArcGIS ecosystem31.  
This 100-meter grid represents the optimal intersection of performance and fidelity for the volumetric renderer. At a 100-meter sampling interval, the geomorphological structure of the Great Bahama Bank is meticulously preserved. The narrow tidal sand ridges and the 1.2-kilometer-wide channels are represented by multiple discrete pixels, preventing the mathematical blending that plagued the 450-meter GEBCO dataset5. Feeding the TCarta 100m SDB depth map into the Beer-Lambert shader will guarantee that the ray marching algorithm correctly detects the 0.5 to 2.0-meter depths, preserving the red-channel transmission and unlocking the photorealistic turquoise backscatter.

## **API Query Architectures for Pipeline Integration**

To integrate this new data paradigm, the rendering pipeline must be overhauled. The script currently responsible for building the bathymetry.json file must transition away from point-based queries against rate-limited APIs, and instead utilize bounding box (BBOX) requests against enterprise GIS image servers. This architecture allows the pipeline to request a continuous raster image (GeoTIFF) in a single, unauthenticated HTTP GET request.

### **Ingesting the 100m SDB via ArcGIS REST Services**

The TCarta 100m SDB layer is hosted on Esri's ArcGIS Online infrastructure under the Item ID f1880f788e384d55af2eaf353d154c0831. Open access Tile Layers and Image Layers on ArcGIS Online are accessible via the ArcGIS REST API. By querying the exportImage or export operation of the associated MapServer/ImageServer, the pipeline can extract the exact bounding box at the native 100-meter resolution.  
Because SDB relies entirely on optical penetration, the sensor cannot detect the seafloor beyond approximately 30 to 40 meters in depth29. Consequently, the TCarta 100m SDB map will contain hard cutoffs and null values (NoData) where the shallow carbonate platforms abruptly plunge into the 4,500-meter depths of the Great Bahama Canyon. To prevent the rendering engine from interpreting deep oceanic trenches as dry land or void space, the pipeline must employ a secondary deep-water base layer to composite beneath the SDB data.

### **Utilizing NOAA ImageServers for the Deep-Water Base**

To acquire the deep-water base layer, the pipeline should query the NOAA NCEI ArcGIS REST ImageServers. NOAA operates powerful, keyless servers that expose their global bathymetric mosaics without rate-limit constraints35. The primary endpoint for continuous merged bathymetry (which utilizes ETOPO 2022 globally) is the DEM\_mosaics/DEM\_all ImageServer35.  
The exportImage operation allows automated scripts to extract a subset of the data by specifying a bounding box. A properly formatted GET request returns a raw TIFF containing the absolute elevation values36.

### **Alternative Protocols: OGC Web Coverage Service (WCS)**

If the pipeline architecture prefers standard Open Geospatial Consortium (OGC) protocols, the GEBCO datasets can be extracted directly via Web Coverage Service (WCS)38. While GEBCO provides Web Map Services (WMS) to serve visual map tiles (e.g., shaded relief images), WMS endpoints do not return raw floating-point elevation data41. WCS endpoints, however, are specifically designed to serve gridded data across the web39.  
GEBCO maintains these services, though their routing architecture was significantly altered in 2024, migrating endpoints to wms.gebco.net/mapserv42. A WCS GetCoverage request can be formulated using parameters equivalent to the ArcGIS REST API, specifying the CoverageName (e.g., GEBCO\_2024), the geographic BBOX, and the FORMAT=GTiff to retrieve the deep-water base map40.

## **Implementation Strategy: The Hybrid Compositing Pipeline**

The path forward to achieving the highest visual fidelity in the Rust/Vulkan volumetric renderer requires abandoning point-by-point elevation lookups in favor of a hybrid, multi-resolution raster compositing strategy.  
The data ingestion script must be rewritten to execute two automated HTTP GET requests. First, it must query the TCarta 100m Satellite Derived Bathymetry dataset via its ArcGIS REST endpoint to acquire the high-resolution shallow water topography. This 100-meter grid provides the necessary spatial frequency to distinctly resolve the 0.5 to 2.0-meter tidal flats, mud banks, and oolitic shoals of the Bahama Banks. Second, the script must query the NOAA DEM\_all ImageServer (or the GEBCO WCS endpoint) to acquire the 450-meter global base map for the same bounding box, returned as a 32-bit floating-point GeoTIFF.  
During the generation of the bathymetry.json artifact (or when generating the underlying mesh/heightmap), the pipeline must composite these two layers. The algorithm should prioritize the SDB depth values for all locations where the SDB data is valid. Where the SDB data hits its optical penetration limit and returns NoData values—such as the vertical drop-off into the Tongue of the Ocean—the script must seamlessly fall back to the 450-meter GEBCO depth values.  
By feeding this hybrid high-resolution grid into the engine, the volumetric water shader will perfectly capture the physical reality of the environment. The ray marching algorithm will encounter the seafloor at 1.0 meter rather than 8.0 meters, preserving the critical transmission of the 670 nm red spectrum. This will organically synthesize the correct photorealistic turquoise coloration of the Bahamian flats without requiring arbitrary calibration hacks or artificial bottom-albedo boosting.
